## 1. Precise zero-density lemma

Let `f` be nonidentically zero and holomorphic on an open set containing a
right half-plane `Re z>=a`. Suppose it has zero exponential type there in
the uniform sense

    for every epsilon>0, |f(z)| <= C_epsilon exp(epsilon |z|).

Then its positive real zeros cannot have positive lower linear density:
there do not exist `d>0,R0` such that their counting function, even without
multiplicity, is at least `d R` for all `R>=R0`.

Reality on the positive axis is not needed for this lemma. It is needed
only in the sign-change application below. The zero function is expressly
excluded.

### Self-contained Green/Carleman proof

Choose a real `b>a` which is not the real part of any zero of `f`, and replace
`f(z)` by `f(z+b)`. Its zeros are discrete, so only countably many values of
`b` are excluded. This preserves zero type and any positive lower density
of sufficiently large real zeros. The new function is holomorphic across
the closed right half-plane, has no zero on its imaginary axis, and does
not vanish at zero. Choose `r0>0` so it has no zero in the closed disk of
radius `r0`. For all sufficiently large radii `R` whose circular arc
contains no zero, put

    D_R={z: Re z>0, r0<|z|<R},
    u(z)=log|f(z)|,
    v_R(z)=Re(1/z-z/R^2)=(1/r-r/R^2)cos(theta).

The function `v_R` is harmonic and positive on `D_R`; it vanishes on the
outer semicircle and the two imaginary-axis segments. The distributional
identity `Delta u=2pi sum_zeta multiplicity(zeta) delta_zeta` and Green's
second identity give

    2pi sum_(zeta in D_R) multiplicity(zeta) v_R(zeta)
      = (2/R) integral_(-pi/2)^(pi/2)
                    u(R exp(i theta)) cos(theta) dtheta
        + integral_(r0<|y|<R) u(iy)(1/y^2-1/R^2) dy
        + I_R.                                             (1)

The inner-circle term is

    I_R=integral_inner [v_R partial_n u-u partial_n v_R] ds.

It is bounded independently of `R`: `u` and its first derivatives are
bounded on that fixed zero-free inner semicircle, and the coefficients
in `v_R` and its derivatives are bounded there. Formula (1) can equivalently
be obtained by removing tiny disks around the zeros and letting their
radii tend to zero. Thus it does not require treating `u` as smooth at a
zero. The generic shift handles boundary zeros without a boundary-limit
assumption.

Use `u(z)<=log C_epsilon+epsilon |z|`, increasing `C_epsilon` to at least
one if necessary. After division by `2pi`, the outer term is bounded by

    2 log C_epsilon/(pi R)+2epsilon/pi.

The constant part of the vertical integral is bounded independently of
`R`. Its remaining part is

    (epsilon/pi) integral_(r0)^R (1/y-y/R^2) dy
       <= (epsilon/pi) log R+O_epsilon(1).

All zeros in `D_R` contribute nonnegatively. Keeping only the positive real
zeros `x_j` therefore yields the required Carleman inequality

    sum_(r0<x_j<R) (1/x_j-x_j/R^2)
       <= (epsilon/pi) log R+O_epsilon(1).             (2)

The constants may depend on `epsilon`, the shifted function and `r0`, but
not on `R`. This is sufficient; no boundedness assumption on `f` is used.

If the positive zero counting function `N(s)` satisfies `N(s)>=d s` for
large `s`, Stieltjes integration by parts gives, up to a bounded initial
contribution,

    sum_(r0<x_j<R) (1/x_j-x_j/R^2)
       = integral_(r0)^R N(s)(1/s^2+1/R^2) ds+O(1)
       >= d log R+O(1).                               (3)

Choose `epsilon<pi d`. Equations (2)--(3) contradict one another along
arbitrarily large allowed radii. This proves the lemma.

This is the classical Green-function mechanism behind the half-plane
Carleman formula, written out to avoid importing an unchecked normalization
or boundary version. The earlier Pila Carlson citation alone is not being
claimed as a citation for this stronger zero-density formulation. No novelty
of Green's identity, the Carleman method, or this classical growth mechanism
is asserted.

## 2. Recurrent real phase rigidity

Let `f` have the preceding holomorphy and zero-type bounds and be real on
the sufficiently large positive real axis. Let `H` be the closure of the
cyclic subgroup generated by `omega` in a compact torus; a translate of
this orbit is equally allowed. Suppose `A:H->R` is continuous and

    f(m)=A(m omega)+o(1)  as integer m tends to infinity.    (4)

Then `A` is constant on `H`.

Suppose instead that `A` is nonconstant. Choose a real `c` strictly between
its minimum and maximum and choose `eta>0` so that both open sets

    U_minus={x: A(x)<c-2eta},
    U_plus ={x: A(x)>c+2eta}

are nonempty. Rotation by `omega` is minimal on `H`. More explicitly, for
either nonempty open `U`, the forward translates `U-j omega`, `j>=0`, cover
`H`. Compactness supplies a finite subcover. Consequently there is an
integer `L` such that every integer block `[n,n+L]` contains a return to
`U_minus` and a return to `U_plus`. This is the precise bounded-gap
(syndetic) property needed here; it holds for finite, rationally resonant
and irrational orbit closures alike.

By (4), for all sufficiently large integer indices the approximation error
is less than `eta`. In every such block, `f-c` therefore takes both a strict
negative and a strict positive value. Since it is real and continuous on
the real interval, it has a real zero between those two indices. Use the
disjoint blocks

    [k(L+1), k(L+1)+L].

Their interiors are separated, so the zeros are distinct and have a
positive lower linear density. The function `f-c` has zero exponential
type and is not identically zero, because these blocks contain both strict
signs. This contradicts Section 1. Hence `A` is constant.

No argument that mere density of the orbit gives frequent sign changes is
used: compactness and minimality provide the required uniform return bound.
Only the approximation along the integer sequence in (4) is required;
uniform phase asymptotics are a sufficient way to obtain it, not an extra
hypothesis of this lemma.

## 3. Every coefficient of a full inverse-power phase expansion is constant

Under the same hypotheses, suppose continuous real functions `A_j` on `H`
satisfy, for every finite integer `J>=0`,

    f(m)=sum_(j=0)^J m^(-j) A_j(m omega)+o(m^(-J)).       (5)

Then every `A_j` is constant on `H`.

Section 2 fixes `A_0=c_0`. Inductively suppose the first `j` coefficients
are constants. The function

    F_j(z)=z^j f(z)-sum_(k=0)^(j-1) c_k z^(j-k)

is holomorphic on the same half-plane, real on its positive real axis and
still of zero exponential type. Equation (5) gives

    F_j(m)=A_j(m omega)+o(1).

Section 2 fixes `A_j` as well. This induction needs no convergence of the
formal inverse-power series and does not claim that `f` equals a convergent
series or a constant function.

Multiplication by a fixed real power `z^kappa` is also legitimate after
restricting to `Re z>=delta>0` and choosing the standard logarithm there.
It preserves reality on the positive real axis and zero exponential type;
the power has only polynomial modulus growth. The preceding arguments
already allow a shifted half-plane. Thus noninteger or negative powers
used to normalize a saddle expansion create no branch-point difficulty
at zero. A fixed positive scalar such as `(2z)^kappa` is equally harmless.

## 4. Exact applicability boundary

The reviewed summable-budget equal-cell product is holomorphic and of zero
exponential type on the right half-plane. It is real on the positive real
axis, even though positivity between integer arities need not be assumed.
Therefore Sections 2--3 apply to any correctly derived real inverse-power
phase expansion of a polynomially normalized such product.

For a proposed ratio of finitely many unequal-cell saddle factors, the
application must separately prove: cancellation of exponential quadratic
and linear resources; the correct polynomial normalization; a full
inverse-power expansion along integer arities; real continuous phase
coefficients on the actual cyclic orbit closure; and a denominator bounded
away from zero after its leading normalization. No theorem here supplies
those source or asymptotic premises automatically.

Even after all coefficients are forced constant, unequal-cell factor
identification does not follow from this lemma. Resonant products can have
nontrivial leading theta cancellations, and an all-order cancellation
classification or a further argument would still be required. Inverse-power
asymptotics also leave terms smaller than every algebraic order unresolved.
For example, the nonzero zero-type function `exp(-z)` has an identically
zero expansion in all inverse powers on the positive axis. This is an
analytic limitation, not an admitted G4 counterexample.

The zero-density, phase-rigidity and all-order coefficient implications
survive this adversarial audit with these hypotheses and boundaries. They
are strictly stronger than checking one adjacent-ratio or leading-theta
condition, but they do not by themselves establish finite persistence,
finite legal forcing, or general G4.
