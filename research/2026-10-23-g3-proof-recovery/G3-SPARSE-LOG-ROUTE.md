## Candidate bypass checked

The strong six-exponentials theorem forbids a rank-one 2-by-3 matrix of the
appropriate algebraic linear combinations of logarithms when its two rows
and three columns have the required algebraic linear independence. See
Waldschmidt, [Variations on the Six Exponentials Theorem, Theorem 2.1](https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~michel.waldschmidt/articles/pdf/Hyderabad.pdf).
The paper's field of algebraic numbers is essential; its PDF text extraction
can lose the overline. No assertion of a newer comprehensive literature audit
or a nonlinear-logarithm equality algorithm is made.

A tempting route is to use the pure parameter r to construct just such a
rank-one matrix from the five supplied logarithms. The needed multiplicative
rectangle does not follow from this curve. The following elementary argument
rules out the direct homogeneous construction even before number theory.

## Polynomial-space lemma

Let K be any characteristic-zero field and

    V = span_K {T_3(t),T_6(t),T_10(t),T_15(t),T_21(t)}.

The basis degrees are 1,4,8,13,19. Their positive differences, each occurring
once, are

    3,4,5,6,7,9,11,12,15,18.

For every nonconstant h in K(t), the vector space

    U_h = {f in V : h*f in V}

has dimension at most one over K.

Proof. Every nonzero element of V has one of the five displayed degrees,
because the basis degrees are distinct. Any two-dimensional subspace has
two elements of different degrees: cancel leading coefficients if necessary.
Write deg h = deg numerator minus deg denominator. If deg h=d is nonzero,
two independent elements of U_h would supply two different pairs of degrees
in the displayed set with the same nonzero difference d. This contradicts
the difference list, also for negative d by reversing the pairs.

If deg h=0, let c in K be its leading-coefficient ratio. Since h is not
constant, h-c is nonzero of negative degree. For f in U_h, both h*f and c*f
belong to V, so (h-c)*f belongs to V. Thus U_h is contained in U_(h-c), and
the preceding nonzero-degree argument again bounds its dimension by one.
This proves the lemma.

## Application to the inherited transcendental-node branch

Take K=Qbar and suppose r is transcendental. Polynomial evaluation at r is
injective over K. Write a=w(1-r), so the five logs span a*V(r). If a rank-one
2-by-3 matrix with entries in this span had independent rows and columns,
choose a nonzero pivot. The ratio of the second-row entry to the first-row
entry in that column defines a rational function h(r). The first-row
polynomial entries f,g in two independent columns are independent in V.
Rank one requires h*f,h*g in V after
evaluation, hence as polynomial identities by transcendence of r. Row
independence makes h nonconstant. The lemma gives a contradiction.

Zero entries do not evade the argument: in a rank-one matrix with two
independent rows and at least two independent columns, neither row is zero;
the outer-product description supplies a nonzero pivot and two independent
column factors. A constant algebraic row ratio violates precisely the
required row independence.

Thus a direct rank-one matrix formed by algebraic homogeneous linear
recombination of these five logs cannot meet the strong theorem's hypotheses
on the proposed pure transcendental branch. This does not exclude adding
further independently justified arithmetic information, a different theorem,
or a nonhomogeneous construction involving 1. Those require actual new
premises; none is supplied here. In particular, the result does not assert
that the strong six-exponentials theorem is useless in every reformulation.

## Continuation: allowing the constant 1 still does not supply a rank-one contradiction

This section is a further authored hand proof, not independent acceptance.
It resolves the explicitly retained nonhomogeneous route check. It does not
claim to exclude applications using additional auxiliary logarithms or other
rank theorems.

Set K=Qbar. In the putative pure branch with transcendental r, write
ell_i=a*T_i(r), where a=w(1-r) is nonzero. The five ell_i are independent
over K because evaluation of the five different-degree polynomials at r is
injective. They are therefore independent over Q. Baker's theorem implies
that 1,ell_1,...,ell_5 are independent over K. In particular,

    1 is not in a*V(r).                                      (B)

The accompanying finite minor certificate proves that the degree-three
monomials in the T_i are independent over K. Consequently a homogeneous
cubic identity in their evaluations forces the corresponding polynomial in
five formal variables to be identically zero. The same holds in degree two.

**Claim.** A rank-one 2-by-3 matrix with entries in
K + span_K{ell_i} cannot simultaneously have two independent rows and three
independent columns over K, using just these premises. Hence adding the
known constant 1 does not produce the hoped-for contradiction to strong
six-exponentials on this branch.

Write the matrix as C+a*B(r), with C a constant K-matrix and each entry of
B(t) in V. Constant invertible row and column operations over K preserve
the required independence and this form. We may put C in its rank normal
form. There are precisely three cases.

### Constant rank two

The matrix is

    [1+a*u,   a*v,   a*w]
    [  a*x, 1+a*y,  a*z],       u,v,w,x,y,z in V(r).

Rank one forces, after division by the nonzero a,

    z+a*(u*z-w*x)=0,    -w+a*(v*z-w*y)=0.

Multiplying these equations by w and z respectively and adding eliminates
the terms without a, giving

    v*z^2 + (u-y)*w*z - x*w^2 = 0.                           (C)

Replace each V-entry by its linear form in five formal variables. Cubic
independence makes (C) a polynomial identity in those variables.

If w and z were dependent linear forms over K, the third column would either
vanish or fix an algebraic ratio between the two rows of a rank-one matrix.
The required row or column independence would fail. They must therefore be
independent, coprime linear forms. Reducing (C) modulo w shows w divides v;
reducing it modulo z shows z divides x. Since the entries are linear forms,

    v=c*w, x=d*z, u-y=d*w-c*z        for some c,d in K.

Substitution in the second displayed minor yields

    a*(c*z-y)=1,

contradicting (B), since c*z-y belongs to V(r). The argument uses the
polynomial identity before evaluation; it does not factor polynomials at
an unknown transcendental numerical point.

### Constant rank one

The matrix is

    [1+a*u, a*v, a*w]
    [  a*x, a*y, a*z].

The last two columns give v*z-w*y=0. Quadratic independence makes this a
formal polynomial identity. If v,w are independent linear forms, their
coprimality gives y=c*v,z=c*w for one c in K. The other two minors become

    v*[c+a*(c*u-x)]=0,  w*[c+a*(c*u-x)]=0.

For c nonzero, either equation contradicts (B). For c=0 the equations force
x=0, and the second row vanishes, violating row independence.

If v,w are dependent but not both zero, write (v,w)=(v0*t,w0*t) for constants
v0,w0 and a nonzero linear form t. The formal identity implies
(y,z)=(v0*s,w0*s) for a linear form s. The second and third columns are then
dependent over K, which violates the required three-column independence.
Finally, if v=w=0, a nonzero y or z forces 1+a*u=0, contradicting (B). If
both are zero, the required column independence already fails. This exhausts
constant rank one, including all zero-entry cases.

### Constant rank zero

This is precisely the homogeneous case proved above by the polynomial-space
lemma. No application of strong six-exponentials is needed in any case.

## Continuation: actual point supply for a natural linear-subgroup construction

This section answers the retained point-dimension question for a specified
family of hyperplanes. It is a new authored hand derivation with finite
representative controls, not independent acceptance or general G3 closure.

Waldschmidt's Theorem 3.1 bounds the K-dimension of H intersect Le^d by
d(d-1) when H contains no nonzero vector from K^d. Here K=Qbar and Le is the
algebraic-linear span of 1 and logarithms of nonzero algebraic numbers. To
exclude a proposed pure input by this theorem, one must actually provide
MORE than that many independent qualifying points in one such hyperplane.
Theorem 2.11 and Corollary 3.2 derive useful consequences from that bound;
they do not grant missing points or closure under products.

Continue with ell_i=a*T_i(r), r transcendental, a nonzero. Set

    V = span_K{T_3,T_6,T_10,T_15,T_21},
    W = K + a*V(r).

Baker's theorem makes W a six-dimensional K-space, with the sum direct.
It is a known subspace of Le. Let E be ANY d-dimensional subspace of V,
with basis e_1,...,e_d, where 1<=d<=5. Consider the hyperplane

    H_E = {z : sum_i e_i(r)*z_i = 0}.

Its coefficients are independent over K by transcendence of r, so its
intersection with K^d is zero, as the primary theorem requires. This family
includes every hyperplane whose independent normal coefficients are
algebraic linear combinations of the five supplied logarithms: their common
nonzero scale a cancels from the hyperplane equation.

The points supplied using ONLY known W-entries form H_E intersect W^d.
The goal is to count this space without assuming a generic value of a.

### Product-space dimension

The fifteen quadratic products of the five basis polynomials are independent.
Therefore multiplication Sym^2(V) -> K[t] is injective. Extending a basis of
E to one of V shows that E*V has dimension

    dim_K(E*V) = 5d - binom(d,2).                             (D)

Indeed the distinct symmetric products with at least one factor in E number
d*(5-d)+d*(d+1)/2. Their only tensor redundancies are the binom(d,2)
antisymmetric pairs within E. This is uniform over all subspaces E, not just
the first d displayed basis elements.

### Special scale values are included

In coordinates z_i=c_i+a*f_i(r), the hyperplane map has image

    E(r) + a*(E*V)(r).

Let s be the dimension of the intersection of these two summands. The first
summand has dimension d, so s<=d, including any nongeneric rational-function
value of a. Using (D), rank-nullity on the six-dimension-per-coordinate
domain gives EXACTLY

    dim_K(H_E intersect W^d) = binom(d,2) + s
                             <= d*(d+1)/2.                  (E)

If a is not in K(r), the intersection is zero: any nonzero common element
would express a as a quotient of two rational functions of r. For an
independent formal scale this gives exactly binom(d,2) points. The upper
bound (E) does not assume this stronger condition on the actual input.

For d=3,4,5 the upper bounds are 6,10,15, whereas a contradiction to the
primary theorem would require at least 7,13,21 independent points. No value
of a, including a special one, can make this construction cross the bound.
For d=2, the previous affine 2-by-3 argument already bounds the qualifying
point space by two, also at the theorem's bound. For d=1 the hyperplane is
zero. Thus every possible independent normal drawn from the supplied log
span is covered.
